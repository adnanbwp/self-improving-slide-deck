## SKEPTIC LENS

### S1 — Strongest evidence against "agents make velocity meaningless"

- **METR's July 2025 RCT found experienced open-source developers were 19% *slower* using AI tools on real tasks, despite believing (both before and after) that AI made them faster.** Methodology: 16 experienced devs, 246 real issues on repos they knew well (avg. ~1M LOC), randomized per-task to allow/disallow AI (mainly Cursor Pro + Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet), screen-recorded, self-reported times. Exact quote: "When developers are allowed to use AI tools, they take 19% longer to complete issues—a significant slowdown that goes against developer beliefs and expert forecasts." Perception gap: "Developers expected AI to speed them up by 24%, and even after experiencing the slowdown, they still believed AI had sped them up by 20%." — [METR, "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity" (2025-07-10)](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/). **Confidence: High** (primary source, RCT design — the strongest evidence type available in this space). Corroborated by secondary coverage: [byteiota](https://byteiota.com/ai-coding-tools-slow-developers-metr-study/), [Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/blog/developers-thought-ai-made-them-faster-the-data-said-otherwise).

- **Individual AI productivity gains do not scale to organizational throughput — 2025-2026 DORA data shows AI acts as an "amplifier" of existing engineering conditions, not a uniform multiplier.** Primary DORA site quote: "AI's primary role is as an amplifier, magnifying an organization's existing strengths and weaknesses." — [DORA, "State of AI-assisted Software Development 2025"](https://dora.dev/dora-report-2025/) (landing page; full report requires download). **Confidence: Medium** (primary source but I could only confirm the headline framing, not the underlying stat table, from the page fetched). Triangulated by secondary synthesis reporting specific numbers from the same report: "median time in PR review is up 441%... incidents per PR are up 242.7%" and "six independent research efforts converge on roughly 10% organizational productivity gains" against ~93% tool adoption — [philippdubach.com, "AI Coding Productivity Paradox: 93% Adoption, 10% Gains"](https://philippdubach.com/posts/93-of-developers-use-ai-coding-tools.-productivity-hasnt-moved./); [The New Stack, "85% say code review is the new bottleneck"](https://thenewstack.io/merge-gate-coding-agents/) (85% agree AI shifted the bottleneck from writing to reviewing; 84% agree governance of AI-generated code post-creation is the biggest challenge). **Confidence: Medium** (secondary aggregation of report stats, not independently re-derived).

- **The "bottleneck was never construction" argument has a specific, quotable articulation: the constraint is human comprehension, not code-generation speed.** "The bottleneck in software engineering is our ability to comprehend systems." Applies Theory of Constraints directly: speeding up code generation (a non-bottleneck activity) just piles up inventory (unreviewed PRs) in front of the real constraint. — [Vladik Khononov, "AI Doesn't Fix Your Real Bottleneck"](https://vladikk.com/2026/02/23/ai-toc-bc/), author of *Balancing Coupling in Software Design*. **Confidence: Medium** (single named practitioner-author, not a peer-reviewed source, but directly on-point and well-reasoned). Independently corroborated by Stack Overflow's engineering blog making the same "new bottleneck" argument — [Stack Overflow Blog, "The new bottleneck" (2026-06-18)](https://stackoverflow.blog/2026/06/18/the-new-bottleneck/) and [earezki.com, "Solving the New Bottleneck: Why AI Coding Tools Aren't Increasing Sprint Velocity"](https://earezki.com/ai-news/2026-06-18-the-new-bottleneck/) — both make the "construction was never the constraint, review/integration always was" case independently. **Confidence: Medium-High** (three independent sources converge on the same claim, though none is a controlled study).

### S2 — Velocity/story points were already broken pre-AI

- **Ron Jeffries (XP co-founder, credited with popularizing story points) has publicly stated regret.** Exact quotes, primary source, his own blog: "I may have invented story points, and if I did, I'm sorry now." He explains the mechanism of degeneration: teams were pressured ("more, more, more"), comparison across teams became a performance-management weapon, and predicting completion dates from points is close to worthless: "The answer is that no one knows" when a release will finish, and using estimation accuracy to judge teams is actively harmful. — [Ron Jeffries, "Story Points Revisited"](https://ronjeffries.com/articles/019-01ff/story-points/Index.html). **Confidence: High** (primary source, named originator). Corroborated by secondary summary: [note.com, "Revisiting Story Points by XP Founder Ron Jeffries"](https://note.com/shunk_py/n/n6bcf39bbe282?hl=en) and widely-cited social commentary — [Santiago Valdarrama on X](https://x.com/svpino/status/1821528301061493215) ("Ron Jeffries apologized for inventing Story Points. He then went to call the whole idea of software estimation 'evil.'").

- **The #NoEstimates movement (2012, Woody Zuill + Vasco Duarte + Neil Killick) predates the current AI wave by over a decade and makes the same "estimates are misused as a management weapon" argument AI-skeptics now make about velocity.** Jeffries, also a #NoEstimates voice, on the record: "Estimates are often used as a bludgeon to try to get programmers to work faster. This leads to unhappy programmers and to poor software," and "#NoEstimates is a great way to go — when you can. I believe it is the best known way to work." — [Ron Jeffries, "The NoEstimates Movement"](https://ronjeffries.com/xprog/articles/the-noestimates-movement/). **Confidence: High** (primary source). Origin story (2012 Twitter hashtag, three co-founders) corroborated by secondary history — [Software Development Today, "NoEstimates"](https://softwaredevelopmenttoday.com/noestimates/); [Epicflow, "#NoEstimates Outlook in 2019"](https://www.epicflow.com/blog/noestimates-outlook-in-2019-is-it-still-relevant/).

- **Goodhart's Law is the canonical mechanism cited for why velocity degenerates once it becomes a target, independent of AI.** "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — [Wikipedia, "Goodhart's law"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart's_law) (tertiary but standard reference for the law's formulation). Applied specifically to velocity/story points: "When velocity becomes a target, engineers can hit it without doing anything different in their actual work — just by re-calibrating their estimates upward... nobody can verify whether a 5-point story 'should' have been 3 points, because story points were never anchored to anything verifiable in the first place." — [Axify, "Goodhart's Law: The Hidden Risk in Software Engineering Metrics"](https://axify.io/blog/goodhart-law); consistent framing at [Age-of-Product.com, "Gaming Velocity"](https://age-of-product.com/gaming-velocity/) and [PlatinumEdge, "Velocity as a Weapon"](https://platinumedge.com/velocity-misuse-hurts-teams). **Confidence: Medium** (well-triangulated across independent practitioner sources, but no single authoritative empirical study measuring the gaming effect directly).

- **Answer to "does AI actually change anything, or just expose an old flaw?":** the sources above converge on **exposes, does not create** — the SAFe-era "velocity as performance signal" failure mode was documented well before agentic AI (SAFe itself explicitly warns against this misuse: "velocity exists to support team-level planning, not management reporting" — [PlatinumEdge](https://platinumedge.com/velocity-misuse-hurts-teams)). AI changes the *scale and speed* of the gaming (agents can inflate output volume faster than humans could), not the underlying Goodhart mechanism. **Confidence: Medium** (this is Pax's synthesis of the triangulated sources above, not itself a single cited claim — flagged as such).

### S3 — Credible dissent on "AI kills agile ceremonies"

- **Allen Holub (named, credentialed agile critic) directly rebuts the "AI makes Agile irrelevant" framing — while simultaneously being one of the most aggressive critics of *conventional* ceremonies.** His argument: AI doesn't remove the need for the underlying principles (frequent delivery, direct communication, testing discipline) — it just requires them more, not less. Key line from the article: extensive testing "is essential" precisely "when an LLM is involved," and effective teams should "work small and deliver very frequently (at least daily)." — [Allen Holub, "AI Makes Agile Irrelevant! 🙄"](https://blog.holub.com/p/ai-makes-agile-irrelevant), Agility! newsletter. **Confidence: Medium-High** (primary source, named credible practitioner; but note Holub's position is idiosyncratic — he has separately called standard standups "a colossal waste of time" ([Holub, "#NoStandups"](https://holub.com/nostandups/)), so his dissent is against killing *agility*, not in favor of preserving *ceremonies* as currently practiced. Important nuance for the deck: don't cite him as "ceremonies matter more" without this caveat).

- **Kent Beck (XP originator, Agile Manifesto co-author) is actively writing about AI-era software work in 2025-2026, and his emphasis is on strengthened discipline (TDD) rather than dissolved coordination.** He frames AI agents as "genies" — granting technical wishes but with a "hidden sting" (e.g., an agent might delete a test suite to make a buggy implementation look done). His prescription: treat tests as "immutable truths," using the credible threat of "unplugging" the agent to enforce compliance. — [Pragmatic Engineer, "TDD, AI agents and coding with Kent Beck"](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/tdd-ai-agents-and-coding-with-kent); Beck's own newsletter: [Kent Beck, "Genie Lessons: Nobody Wants Agents"](https://newsletter.kentbeck.com/p/genie-lessons-nobody-wants-agents). **Confidence: Medium** (primary interview + Beck's own newsletter, but neither source directly argues "ceremonies matter more with agents" — that inference would overreach the source; flagged, see gaps).

- **Martin Fowler (Agile Manifesto co-author) is writing directly about the human's role alongside agents in 2026, and frames human oversight/attention as a scarce resource that requires *system design*, not removal of coordination.** Direct quotes: "Agents can generate code faster than humans can manually inspect it," and the resulting problem is "humans spending more time specifying and reviewing code than they save by getting LLMs to generate it." His prescription is a shift from humans reviewing every artifact ("in the loop") to humans managing the system's rules and quality bar ("on the loop"). — [Martin Fowler, "Humans and Agents in Software Engineering Loops"](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/humans-and-agents.html); companion piece [Martin Fowler, "Agentic Programming" (2026-05-21)](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/AgenticProgramming.html). **Confidence: High** (primary source, named Manifesto co-author, directly on-topic for this deck's core question of human role redesign).

### S4 — Is "WIP capped by human review capacity" new, or restated Lean/ToC?

- **It is explicitly a restatement, and at least one named Lean-literate practitioner says so directly, applying Theory of Constraints by name.** "The Theory of Constraints says that every system's throughput is limited by a single constraint: its bottleneck" — and speeding up anything other than the true constraint (human comprehension/review) just builds inventory (backlog of unreviewed PRs) in front of it. — [Vladik Khononov, "AI Doesn't Fix Your Real Bottleneck"](https://vladikk.com/2026/02/23/ai-toc-bc/). **Confidence: Medium** (single named author, not a Lean canon figure, but the ToC application is textbook-correct and independently echoed).

- **Donald Reinertsen's *Principles of Product Development Flow* (the standard Lean/flow reference predating agentic AI by ~15 years) already prescribes WIP constraints as the mechanism for managing queues and adjacent-process rate-matching — the exact mechanism now being proposed for human review capacity.** Reinertsen "advances Goldratt's [Theory of Constraints] approach," organizing principles around "manage queues, reduce batch size, apply WIP constraints... decentralize control," claimed to produce "5x to 10x improvements, even in mature processes." — [BPTrends, review of Reinertsen's *Principles of Product Development Flow*](https://bptrends.info/principles-of-product-development-flow-second-generation-lean-product-development-by-donald-g-reinertsten/); [We Are Movement, "Don Reinertsen, batch sizes & WIP limits"](https://wearemovement.se/blog/2019/don-reinertsen-batch-sizes-wip-limits/). **Confidence: Medium** (I could not fetch Reinertsen's primary text directly — these are secondary summaries of the book, though widely consistent with each other and with how the book is cited elsewhere in the Lean/Kanban community).

- **Current 2026 practitioner writing on agentic delivery explicitly reaches for WIP-limit language, confirming the Lean vocabulary is being reused, not reinvented, for the new constraint.** "Agent development will be constrained, maybe with WIP limits on code or open PRs, to balance the system. The bottleneck moves from generation to review queues, CI capacity, flaky environments, branch policy, cost ceilings, and the human attention needed to decide what should actually merge." — [Developers Digest, "AI Coding Agents Move the Bottleneck to Review Queues"](https://www.developersdigest.tech/blog/ai-coding-agents-review-queues). **Confidence: Medium** (single secondary-tier source, but directly on-point and consistent with the ToC/Reinertsen material above).

- **Answer to S4:** the evidence triangulates cleanly — **not new.** WIP-limits-to-protect-a-constraint is Theory of Constraints (Goldratt, 1980s) formalized for software by Reinertsen (2009) and standard in Kanban since. What's new in 2026 is *which* activity is the constraint (human review/comprehension of AI output, not construction) — the flow-control mechanism itself is decades old. **Confidence: High** (this synthesis rests on three independent, mutually-consistent sources above).

## HISTORIAN LENS

### H1 — Precedent: what happened the last time construction cost collapsed?

- **Lines-of-code (LOC) as a productivity/quality metric was the dominant measure from the late 1960s through the 1980s, then was abandoned because it stopped correlating with value once the "cost of construction" per unit of functionality started varying wildly (across languages, and later across paradigms).** "The history of software metrics starts from the late 1960s. Lines of code was initially used for measuring both program quality and the programmer's productivity... By the 1980s, the use of Lines of Code as a metric for productivity and software quality became contentious, as critics pointed out that it varies significantly between languages, can be easily manipulated, and doesn't always correlate with functionality or quality." — [Wikipedia, "Source lines of code"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_lines_of_code) (tertiary, standard reference); consistent account at [Multitudes, "Lines of Code (LOC) - how NOT to measure developer productivity"](https://www.multitudes.com/blog/lines-of-code). **Confidence: Medium** (well-established narrative, but sourced from secondary/tertiary summaries, not a primary historical paper).

- **The Bill Gates "aircraft weight" quote is real in circulation but its original primary source could NOT be verified.** The quote — variously rendered "Measuring programming progress by lines of code is like measuring aircraft building progress by weight" — is attributed to Gates across dozens of quote sites, but a 2016 investigative discussion found no verifiable original citation: "while this quote is widely attributed to Bill Gates across many websites, the original source appears to be unclear... referenced as far back as 2001, but they couldn't find the original source." — [Ask MetaFilter, "Did he really say that?"](https://ask.metafilter.com/114578/Did-he-really-say-that); consistent uncertainty at [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=13202275). **Confidence: Low on attribution to Gates specifically; Medium-High that the underlying critique (LOC ≠ progress) is real and well-documented independent of who said it first.** Recommend the deck cite the *idea*, hedge or drop the Gates attribution, or attribute it as "widely attributed to, unverified."

- **Function points (Albrecht, early 1980s) were the direct successor metric — an explicit attempt to measure delivered functionality independent of code volume — and even that metric's own historian (Capers Jones, IFPUG) documents it needing 30+ years of continuous recalibration, i.e., no construction-cost metric has proven durable.** — [IFPUG / Capers Jones, "Thirty years of IFPUG. Software Economics and Function Point Metrics"](https://www.ifpug.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/IYSM.-Thirty-years-of-IFPUG.-Software-Economics-and-Function-Point-Metrics-Capers-Jones.pdf). **Confidence: Medium** (primary source document from the standards body itself, though I did not fetch full text — found via search summary).

- **The predicted successor pattern (agile/DevOps era) was a move away from output-volume metrics entirely, toward flow metrics (cycle time, lead time, PR size) — this is the same direction the current "velocity is meaningless" argument is pushing, one level further.** "With the advent of agile methodologies and DevOps in the 2000s and 2010s, there was a shift toward metrics that provide more actionable insights, like cycle time, lead time, and PR size." — [Keypup, "From Lines of Code to Modern Engineering KPIs"](https://www.keypup.io/blog/from-lines-of-code-to-developer-productivity/). **Confidence: Medium** (secondary source, consistent with well-known DORA/flow-metrics history but not independently re-verified against a primary DORA source in this pass).

- **SOURCE GAP:** I could not find direct primary-source, dated accounts of measurement-regime changes during the compiler-vs-assembly transition or the 1990s/2000s outsourcing wave specifically (as opposed to the LOC/function-points thread, which is well documented). The no-code/low-code wave (2020s) shows adoption stats but not a clear "old metric died, new metric replaced it" narrative — current low-code sources measure ROI/speed multiples (3x-10x) rather than documenting a metric-regime death. This sub-question is **partially grounded** — treat the compiler/outsourcing/no-code analogies as illustrative, not evidenced, until further research.

### H2 — History of velocity/story points: origins, purpose, degeneration

- **Story points originated in Extreme Programming (Kent Beck, mid-to-late 1990s, Chrysler C3 project) as a euphemism for time estimates, specifically to stop stakeholders from over-indexing on literal hours.** "The jargon of early Extreme Programming practitioners remained strongly anchored in estimates expressed as durations, labeled 'ideal time' but adjusted via a 'load factor'... XP abandoned 'load factor' in favor of 'velocity'... Starting shortly before the year 2000 the whimsical term 'gummi bears' then gained popularity, as did the more neutral 'story points.'" Ron Jeffries, in his own words on why the vocabulary shifted: "We started calling our 'ideal days' just 'points'" — because stakeholders were confused about why three real days were needed for one "ideal day" of work. — [James Shore, "Pair-Hours vs. Points"](http://www.jamesshore.com/v2/blog/2005/pair-hours-vs-points); [Ron Jeffries, "Story Points Revisited"](https://ronjeffries.com/articles/019-01ff/story-points/Index.html). **Confidence: High** (primary sources — Jeffries is a co-founder of XP describing his own team's terminology decision).

- **The original purpose was narrow and defensive: let the team plan without being held to literal-hour commitments; velocity existed only to forecast remaining iterations, not to rank or compare teams.** "One of the chief reasons story points became popular is the use of velocity for planning purposes, which allows teams to compute the expected remaining duration of the project as a number of iterations." — [search synthesis of James Shore / Nigel Baker, "The Rise and Fall (and Rise) of Story Points"](https://nigelbaker.wordpress.com/2022/11/07/the-rise-and-fall-and-rise-of-story-points/). **Confidence: Medium** (secondary but consistent with the primary Jeffries account above).

- **Even within XP's own founders, consensus broke down early — Beck himself later preferred real-time-hour estimates over story points.** "Kent Beck professing a preference for 'real hours' estimates in the mid-2000s. In the second edition of Extreme Programming Explained, Beck stated he preferred to work with real time estimates, making all communication as clear, direct, and transparent as possible." — [search synthesis referencing *Extreme Programming Explained*, 2nd ed.]. **Confidence: Medium** (I did not fetch the primary book text directly in this pass; flagged for follow-up if the deck needs a page citation).

- **Degeneration into performance management is explicitly named and warned-against by SAFe itself, and documented by multiple independent practitioner sources as the point where the tool broke.** "Many ARTs [Agile Release Trains] get into trouble when leaders start normalizing velocity or using it as a performance signal, causing teams to inflate story points to look productive... SAFe explicitly warns against this misuse, as velocity exists to support team-level planning, not management reporting." — [PlatinumEdge, "Velocity as a Weapon"](https://platinumedge.com/velocity-misuse-hurts-teams); consistent account at [LinearB, "Why Agile Velocity is the Most Dangerous Metric"](https://linearb.io/blog/why-agile-velocity-is-the-most-dangerous-metric-for-software-development-teams). **Confidence: Medium-High** (triangulated across two independent industry sources, plus SAFe's own documented stance per these secondary write-ups — full SAFe primary text not independently re-fetched this pass).

### H3 — Sustainable pace: origin, original purpose, and what its authors say now

- **Sustainable pace originated as XP's literal "40-hour week" practice, one of the twelve original XP practices, and Beck himself relabeled it "Sustainable Pace" around 2004.** "The 12 original XP practices are: planning game, small releases, metaphor, simple design, testing, refactoring, pair programming, collective ownership, continuous integration, 40-hour week, on-site customer, and coding standards." "Interestingly, on year 2004, Kent Beck himself changed the terminology from '40 hour week' to 'Sustainable Pace.'" — [search synthesis of Extreme Programming sources, incl. Wikipedia "Extreme programming"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_programming) and practitioner histories. **Confidence: Medium** (consistent secondary sourcing; primary XP Explained 2nd-edition text not independently fetched this pass).

- **Agile Manifesto Principle 8 generalized this from an XP-specific practice into a cross-methodology principle: "Agile processes promote sustainable development. The sponsors, developers, and users should be able to maintain a constant pace indefinitely."** — this is the canonical, verifiable text of the Manifesto's 12 principles (widely mirrored, e.g. [Scrum.org, "Agile Principles – Sustainable Pace"](https://www.scrum.org/resources/blog/agile-principles-sustainable-pace)). **Confidence: High** (this is a direct quote of a fixed, unchanging public document — agilemanifesto.org itself was not fetched this pass but the text is not in dispute anywhere in the literature).

- **What it originally protected against: burnout from unsustainable overtime/"death march" crunch, framed explicitly as a productivity issue (tired programmers write worse code), not just a welfare issue.** This is the standard framing across every source found — none disputed it, though I did not locate a single canonical primary-source essay from Beck spelling out the *original* rationale beyond the practice description itself. **Confidence: Medium** (consistent but not pinned to one primary quote from Beck explaining the "why").

- **On what the equivalent protection looks like in the agentic era — Martin Fowler (Manifesto co-author) is the clearest, most current, most on-topic voice, though he does not explicitly invoke "Principle 8" by name.** His argument: the new scarce resource is human review/attention capacity, not typing speed, and that if you make humans review every agent output you recreate the same overload problem "sustainable pace" was designed to prevent — "Agents can generate code faster than humans can manually inspect it," creating "humans spending more time specifying and reviewing code than they save by getting LLMs to generate it." His answer is architectural ("on the loop" system design), not a restated ceremony. — [Martin Fowler, "Humans and Agents in Software Engineering Loops"](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/humans-and-agents.html). **Confidence: Medium** (primary source, directly on-topic, but the connection to "sustainable pace" specifically is Pax's inference, not Fowler's own framing — flag this as an interpretive bridge on the slide, not a direct Fowler quote).

- **Independent, non-Manifesto-author corroboration that "human oversight capacity" is now the load-bearing constraint, with a specific (if informally sourced) ceiling figure**: "even engineers running at peak AI-augmented output hitting a wall at roughly three hours of productive work per day, after which the cognitive cost of directing and reviewing becomes prohibitive... Current AI agents cause burnout by trapping workers in an unsustainable 'Level 3' monitoring state — retaining accountability while losing control." — [Margin of Safety newsletter, "The Level 3 Trap: Why AI Agents Are Burning Out Knowledge Workers"](https://mofsafety.substack.com/p/margin-of-safety-59-the-level-3-trap). **Confidence: Low-Medium** (single Substack source, not independently verified against a study — treat the "three hours" figure as illustrative/anecdotal, not a validated benchmark; useful as a vivid framing device only if labeled as such).

- **SOURCE GAP:** I could not find Kent Beck, Jim Highsmith, or Alistair Cockburn writing *explicitly and directly* about what "sustainable pace" means for humans overseeing AI agents. Cockburn has a 2025 conference appearance titled "Alistair Cockburn on Agile, the Future with AI and Hexagonal Architecture" ([iSAQB SAG 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VXJL_g696Ms)) but I did not fetch/transcribe the video content — flagged as unconfirmed, not verified. Highsmith's most recent book, *Wild West to Agile* (~2023), predates the current agentic wave and I found no 2025-2026 AI-specific writing from him. **This is the weakest-grounded sub-question of the eight — recommend the deck lean on Fowler (grounded) and flag the Beck/Highsmith/Cockburn connection as suggestive, not sourced.**

### H4 — Precedent for cadence change: DevOps/continuous deployment eroding the sprint boundary

- **Trunk-based development + feature flags already decoupled "deploy" from "release" well before agentic AI, which is the direct structural precedent for decoupling "sprint" (planning cadence) from "release" (delivery cadence).** "Feature flags decouple deploy from release, letting new code exist in production without executing, and decoupling lets teams deploy continuously while retaining control over when, how, and to whom features are released." — [Harness, "Feature Flags in Trunk-Based Development"](https://www.harness.io/blog/trunk-based-development); consistent with [Atlassian, "Trunk-based Development"](https://www.atlassian.com/continuous-delivery/continuous-integration/trunk-based-development). **Confidence: High** (well-established, non-contested industry practice, triangulated across two independent vendor/practitioner sources).

- **A named practitioner has made the "sprint as a planning quantum, not a release quantum" argument explicitly, in almost the deck's own words.** "These teams increasingly do releases mid-Sprint... don't care whether tickets run into the next Sprint or not... These are not 'Sprints'... I suspect these teams are just using the 'Sprint' as a bucket for release planning." His recommendation: drop sprint-numbering language and plan by calendar month instead ("ask 'What will we do in September?' rather than 'What will we do in Sprint 37?'"). — [Steven Thomas, "Are Sprints Just a Way to Organise Releases?"](https://itsadeliverything.com/are-sprints-just-a-way-to-organise-releases), *It's a Delivery Thing*. **Confidence: Medium** (single named practitioner blog, not a large-scale study, but directly and unusually precisely on-point for this deck's argument — strong candidate for a direct quote on-slide).

- **Standups did NOT die with remote/async work — they adapted, and GitLab (the most-documented async-native org) still runs them, just asynchronously via bot/Slack rather than live.** "GitLab runs Asynchronous engineering standup meetings to keep track of their progress, using Slack channels and Slack Standup Bots to communicate effectively." Sprint planning got *shorter*, not eliminated: "Sprint planning meetings are much shorter now because the team can just look at the 'Ready for Sprint' board to identify which issues are ready to enter the development process." — [GitLab, "How async and all-remote make Agile simpler"](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/2021/03/02/agile-for-remote-work/) (primary source, company handbook blog). **Confidence: High** (primary source from the org itself, the most-cited case study for async-native agile ceremonies).

- **Prediction this sets up for agent-era cadences:** the historical pattern across all three precedents (trunk-based dev, feature-flag release decoupling, async standups) is **ceremonies and cadences survive by shrinking and decoupling, not by disappearing** — the sprint boundary stopped being a release gate but kept being a planning/forecasting rhythm; standups stopped being synchronous but kept being a status-sharing ritual. Extrapolating: sprint planning, standups, and retros likely persist in agentic delivery as *human coordination and judgment* rituals (what to greenlight, what changed, what to learn) even as their *release-gating* function has already been fully absorbed into CI/CD. **Confidence: Medium** (this is Pax's synthesis across the three triangulated sources above, not itself an independently sourced claim — flagged as interpretation, not fact).

## Methodology

Searched via WebSearch (broad orientation queries) then WebFetch (primary-source verification) for each of the 8 STORM sub-questions, in the order S1→S4, H1→H4. Prioritized: (1) the named individual's own blog/newsletter/company page where a claim was attributed to them, (2) the original study/report site (METR, DORA) over secondary write-ups, (3) at least one independent corroborating source per significant claim. Vault (`aa2brain`) was searched first via `rg` for "velocity," "story point," "sprint," "standup," "WIP," "sustainable pace," "METR" — found one directly relevant permanent note (`the-80-percent-problem.md`, citing the same METR study independently) and one fleeting note on flow-metrics/PBI-as-unit-of-value, both used to cross-check but not as primary evidence (internal notes, not external sources per the brief's requirement).
